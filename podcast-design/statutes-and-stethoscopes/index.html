Element 01

Background

Your palette, so the channel reads as one body of work.

Element 02

Face

A real still from the conversation, with an expression that matches the promise in the text.

Element 03

Text

Three to five words, one of them highlighted, written to open a question rather than answer it.

The North Star
Before anything gets designed, we decide the single most compelling idea in the episode. That idea becomes the North Star, and then the whole package points at it: the thumbnail, the title, the clips, the show notes. When the packaging pulls in one direction instead of four, the click rate moves.
Curiosity, not clickbait
The distinction we hold to is simple. Curiosity means the thumbnail makes you want to know the answer and the episode actually delivers it. Clickbait means the thumbnail promises something the episode never pays off. The second one buys a click and costs you a subscriber, so we only ever write to what is genuinely in the conversation.
Where the room is
The strongest performers in this niche are still running plain designs. That is an opening. A conservative audience does not mean conservative packaging has to win, it means nobody has tested properly yet.

Three concepts for the first episodes. Hover any of them to enlarge.

Concept 1

The question a listener already has, made visual. Two faces, and the thin file sitting beside the one that should have arrived.

Concept 2

The redacted record carries the curiosity and the expression finishes it. The guest sits inset so the chart stays the hero.

Concept 3

A statement rather than a question, staged as an exhibit. The torn hole in the chart is the whole idea in one image.

Your Differentiator

“The medical-legal podcast hosted by someone who has actually sat on both sides of the chart, as a physician assistant and as a trial attorney.”

We checked 21 podcasts in your space before writing this, and every claim below points at a named show you can go and listen to.

What Competitors Do
  • Lawyers talking to lawyers. Trial Lawyer Nation, The Jury Is Out and Just Verdicts are all hosted by trial attorneys. They teach trial craft well, and none of them has ever written in a medical chart.
  • Clinicians talking to clinicians. Legal Nurse Podcast and Doctors and Litigation know the medicine, but they are speaking to other medical professionals rather than to the attorneys who hire them.
  • The one direct competitor with a podcast points it at the expert. Round Table Group places expert witnesses, the same as you do, and their show Engaging Experts interviews those experts. After 186 episodes it has one rating on Apple.
What Statutes & Stethoscopes Does
  • Points the microphone at the attorney rather than the expert, so the listener leaves with something they can use
  • Can put a medical record in front of the audience and explain what it means, because the host has written in one. No other show in this space can do that
  • Puts a guest attorney and a clinical perspective in the same conversation, hosted by somebody who has worked both jobs
  • Gives the audience somewhere to go, which turns listening into case submissions

  • Ep 2, Josh Perry: “What is it that you as a medical attorney look for in a medical expert when you're hiring someone?”
  • Ep 4, Mark Tolles II: “When you're looking for a medical expert, what are some red flags that would keep you from hiring one?”

This is your ignition question. Ask it in every episode.

Interview Lanes: Where We Would Take It

Four lanes to pull from in any episode rather than a running order, with two or three of them usually landing in a single conversation.

Lane 01

The Expert Who Did Not Work Out

Your question, asked from the other end. Ask what a good expert looks like and you get a list. Ask about one that went wrong and you get a story.

  • Tell me about an expert you hired who did not work out. What did you miss?
  • What does an expert do on the stand that makes your heart sink?
  • Have you ever dropped an expert in the middle of a case?
  • What do you ask every expert now that you never used to ask?
Lane 02

Inside the Record

Nobody else can host this. You have written in a chart and you have cross-examined one.

  • You open a nursing home chart. What do you look at first?
  • What gets written in a chart that means the opposite of what it says?
  • Where do you usually find the thing the other side hoped nobody would spot?
  • How can you tell when a record has been tidied up after the fact?
Lane 03

What the Case Was Worth

One of your guests turned a five thousand dollar offer into forty thousand. It got one line at the very end of a 53 minute episode. That is an episode on its own.

  • Tell me about a case another firm nearly gave away. What did they miss?
  • What in the medical records moved the number the most?
  • When you turn a case down, what is the real reason?
  • How do you value a case when the client has outlived their prognosis?
Lane 04

How It Got This Way

You went here once in seven episodes and it is the most distinctive material on the channel. It also travels furthest beyond your current audience.

  • What has corporate ownership changed about care in these facilities?
  • Electronic records were supposed to make things clearer. What did they make worse?
  • What does staffing actually look like on the days these injuries happen?
  • If you could change one rule about how records are kept, what would it be?

Show Feature Ideas

We looked at what is already running in your space first, and across the 21 shows we checked, only two of them run a named recurring feature.

Part One: What Your Competitors Are Doing

Works, and it is the best example

Trial Lawyer Nation, “Ask Michael & Malorie Anything”

A named listener question feature. People email their questions in and the hosts answer a batch in the episode.

Signal: the biggest attorney show we found. 210 episodes, running nine years, 4.8 stars from around 192 ratings, and it charts at number 84 in Apple US Business and Careers.

Worked, then stopped

Physicians Helping Attorneys, listener Q&A

Two physicians selling medical-legal consulting to injury attorneys, running a listener question episode roughly every six months. Structurally the closest thing to your business that exists.

Signal: 74 episodes, then nothing since July 2024. The format worked. The publishing schedule is what beat them.

Format discipline, not a segment

The Great Trials Podcast

No listener feature, but every single episode title carries the verdict amount: “Hernandez v. Colony Capital LLC, $110.2 Million Verdict.”

Signal: 4.7 stars from 133 ratings across eight years. The number in the title tells you the payoff before you press play.

The cautionary one

Engaging Experts

Round Table Group's show. Every episode is an expert being introduced, with a title that reads like a directory entry.

Signal: 186 episodes and one rating on Apple. Interviewing the expert instead of teaching the attorney is a real risk, and this is what it looks like at scale.

Part Two: Ideas We Can Implement

Two of these borrow from something already proven, and two go where nobody in this space has gone yet.

Borrowed, and it doubles as lead generation

The Chart Question

A short segment where you take one question about reading a medical record and answer it in the episode. Trial Lawyer Nation has already proven listeners engage with this format.

How we run it safely. We write the questions in-house rather than inviting attorneys to send in live case material, because a lawyer who posts details of an active matter can breach their own client's confidentiality. You get the same segment with none of that risk, and the questions are better because we write them to match what people are actually searching for.

Once the show has an audience, we can open it to genuine listener questions with a properly worded submission form.

Prompt to generate chart questions
You are helping write a recurring podcast segment called "The Chart Question" for a US medical-legal podcast hosted by a physician assistant turned trial attorney. The audience is plaintiff and defence trial attorneys handling medical malpractice, personal injury, nursing home negligence and wrongful death. Generate 10 questions an attorney would genuinely ask about reading and using medical records in litigation. Rules: - Every question must be GENERIC. No real case, no real person, no real facility, no dates, no jurisdictions. - Written from the attorney's point of view, in the words an attorney would actually use. - Each must be answerable in 2 to 3 minutes in an episode. - Focus on practical mechanics: what to look for, what a document means, how to spot a gap, when to bring in an expert. - Prioritise topics attorneys search for: expert witness depositions, standard of care, causation, record chronologies, cross examination of a medical expert. - Avoid anything requiring state-specific legal advice. For each question give: the question itself, the one clinical or procedural point the answer should land, and a suggested episode title under 60 characters.
Open opportunity, nobody does this

Red Flag Round

A fictional record excerpt goes in front of the guest and they call the red flag inside 60 seconds. This one is for clinician guests, so it starts once medical guests are on the show. No show in this space reads a record out to its audience, it needs both of your credentials to host, and it clips beautifully.

How we run it safely. The records are written from scratch, so there is no real patient and no real case anywhere near it. That removes the privacy and confidentiality question entirely, and it is cleaner than trying to strip identifiers out of a real chart. You say in the episode that the record is fictional, which also makes the segment easier to follow.

Prompt to generate a synthetic record excerpt
Write a FICTIONAL medical record excerpt for a podcast segment called "Red Flag Round". Nothing in it may correspond to a real patient, clinician, facility or case. Setting: a US skilled nursing facility. [Swap for hospital, ED or home health as needed.] Produce a 150 to 250 word excerpt containing: - Nursing notes across 3 to 4 shifts, written in realistic clinical shorthand and abbreviations - A vital signs table with plausible values - At least one medication administration entry - ONE clear clinical red flag that a trained clinician spots and a layperson misses. Options: a deteriorating trend documented but never escalated, a missing reassessment after a fall, a pressure injury staged inconsistently between entries, a held medication with no reason recorded, or a note timed after the event it describes. - One or two harmless details that look alarming but are not, so the guest has to discriminate. Use obviously invented names (Resident A, Nurse B) or clearly fictional ones. No real dates, use Day 1, Day 2. No real facilities or towns. Then, separately and labelled "PRODUCER NOTES, DO NOT READ OUT": state the red flag, why it matters in litigation, and the two decoys. Everything must be clinically plausible enough that a nurse or physician would find it credible.
Open opportunity

Your Signature Last Question

One question you ask every guest, right at the end, forever.

“What's the one thing in a medical record that attorneys keep missing?”

None of the 21 shows we checked has a signature closing question. It builds a library that gets more valuable the longer the show runs, and after a year you have a compilation episode that costs nothing to make.
